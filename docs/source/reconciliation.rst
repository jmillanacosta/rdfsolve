Reconciling a dataset with its authority sources
================================================

A dataset (any RDF, or a table mapped to RDF) states facts for which other sources are the
authority: a protein database for proteins, a chemical database for compound identifiers, a
reaction database for reactions. In reconciliation, the dataset's statements are compared
with those of its authorities; agreement, addition and conflict are found, a decision is
taken by a stated policy, and the result is emitted. Every artifact is RDF in community
standards, so that it can be read and run again without rdfsolve.

The approach is validated by simulation on three use cases (below) before any artifact is
signed or published.

Steps
-----

The same five steps apply to every kind of statement (identities, types, edges):

#. **Gather**: the dataset's statements about the terms in scope are collected.
#. **Ask**: each authority is asked one question per source; the answer is a table.
#. **Compare**: statements are compared in the target model, into which every source,
   authorities included, is converted by its rule, and joined through the mapping set.
   Each statement is found to agree, to be added, to be contradicted, or to have no
   evidence.
#. **Decide**: each statement is decided by the policy, and the reason is recorded.
#. **Emit**: a patch to the source, the target-model graph converted from the source and the
   patch, and a report computed from the decisions are written.

Artifacts
---------

Each artifact is a nanopublication (Groth et al. 2010): an assertion graph, its provenance,
and publication information, identified by a trusty URI (Kuhn and Dumontier 2014). Bulk
results are not nanopublications. They form a dataset that is regenerated by the
application and pinned by a checksum. Only fixes and conflicts are given their own claim
nanopublications, grouped by nanopublication indexes (Kuhn et al. 2015).

.. list-table::
   :header-rows: 1
   :widths: 16 34 50

   * - Artifact
     - Holds
     - Standard
   * - Plan
     - The steps in order, each pointing to its rule
     - P-Plan over PROV (Garijo and Gil 2012), as in OpenPREDICT (Celebi et al. 2020)
   * - Rule
     - A conversion, subset or check, over any entity, with the scope as a parameter
     - SPARQL executable (``sh:SPARQLConstructExecutable``, ``schema:target``), as in SIB's
       collection of queries (Emonet et al. 2025); for tables, RML (Dimou et al. 2014)
       written in YARRRML (Heyvaert et al. 2018)
   * - Policy
     - The authority per namespace, or per predicate with subject and object categories;
       uniqueness assumptions; accepted match levels; preferred entries
     - SHACL-style targets (``sh:targetClass``, ``sh:targetSubjectsOf``)
   * - Application
     - A rule, the source release, the scope binding, the input mapping sets, and the
       completeness of each batch
     - PROV; completeness as a ``dqv:QualityMeasurement`` (W3C DQV; Zaveri et al. 2016)
   * - Mapping claim
     - That two identifiers name the same thing, and how this was found
     - SSSOM (Matentzoglu et al. 2022) in its OWL form (``owl:Axiom``), with a semapv
       justification
   * - Decision
     - The claims accepted or rejected, the policy applied, the evidence, and the patch
     - CiTO ``agreesWith`` / ``disagreesWith`` (Peroni and Shotton 2012), after the claim,
       support and challenge model of micropublications (Clark et al. 2014); ECO (Nadendla
       et al. 2022); OWL 2 negative assertions under the conditions below

Rules
-----

A rule is written in plain SPARQL 1.1, with its prefixes and explicit graphs, so that it is
run by any engine. The scope (the records it is applied to) is a declared parameter, bound
by the application; it is never written into the rule as ``VALUES``. The query that is
executed (scoped and batched) is kept with the application and in the session; the rule
holds the portable form. A rule is accepted when it gives the same results on two engines
(Oxigraph and QLever).

Tables are mapped with RML, written in YARRRML and published as the RML produced by the
translator. Not all of the modular RML is covered by the translators (RML-Core and RML-CC
are not covered by YATTER), so a mapping is limited to features that survive the YARRRML to
RML round trip; it is run by Morph-KGC. Each column's namespace (a Bioregistry prefix), the
table's release and checksum are declared, and each row's identity is kept, so that a fix is
traced to its row.

Identity
--------

Identity claims are SSSOM mappings. The predicate states what the evidence supports:
``skos:exactMatch``, or ``skos:closeMatch``, ``skos:broadMatch`` or ``skos:narrowMatch``.
The justification is a semapv term: ``semapv:MappingChaining`` for a mapping found through
another identifier, with the chained mappings recorded (the ``derived_from`` field proposed
for SSSOM). A chain is sound only through exact matches (``closeMatch`` followed by
``exactMatch`` is not ``exactMatch``). An automatic decision on a stated mapping is a
``semapv:MappingReview``, with the reviewing tool recorded (``mapping_tool``), never a
reviewer (``reviewer_id``), which names a person.

A cross-reference (``oboInOwl:hasDbXref``) is not an exact match. When an authority's
cross-references are taken as exact, this is stated as an assumption of the policy.

Rules that combine sources are joined through a mapping set, given to the application as an
input graph, so that every rule stays plain SPARQL and identity is held only in mapping
claims. SeMRA (Hoyt et al. 2025) is not a dependency, since the issuer of a namespace is not
taken as its authority by its prioritization (see related tools). SSSOM is read and written
so that SeMRA and other tools can be used on the result.

Comparison and conflicts
------------------------

Statements are compared in the target model, after each source's rule. A rule from an
authority into the target model is written once and reused for every dataset. Statements
over-generated by a rule (every pair across a reaction's two sides) add only silence, never
conflict.

No conflict exists in RDF by itself: an edge is not excluded by another edge. A conflict is a
violation of the target model's SHACL shapes (the profile's shapes, with the constraints
declared by the policy) over the union of the claims. Conflicts are detected in a closed
world (SHACL); an authority's exclusion is recorded in an open world (OWL), as follows.

Evidence, completeness and negative statements
----------------------------------------------

- Evidence is given as an ECO term. An authority's own evidence (for example, the evidence
  of a protein annotation) is kept with its claim. An automatic decision is given
  ``ECO:0000501`` (evidence used in automatic assertion), or a more specific term where one
  exists.
- Completeness is recorded for each batch of a question. Silence is meaningful only for
  records in a complete batch: it is then recorded as no evidence found, in the sense of
  ``ECO:0000307`` (defined for manual assertion; an equivalent for automatic assertion is
  required before use). Silence is never recorded as a negative statement.
- A negative statement (OWL 2 ``NegativeObjectPropertyAssertion``, or a class assertion of a
  complement) is written only when the authority's answer is complete and the authority's
  links for that namespace are declared unique by the policy. One CAS number can be listed by
  several chemical entries (salts, forms); without the uniqueness assumption, a claim is not
  excluded by another answer.

Result
------

The result is a patch to the source, held as data: a graph of statements added and a graph
of statements removed, both ground, as in the RDF difference of Berners-Lee and Connolly
(Delta). A SPARQL 1.1 Update (``DELETE DATA``, ``INSERT DATA``) is generated from the patch,
so that it can be applied to any copy of the source. RML is not used for the patch, since
triples are generated by RML but never removed.

A decision is written into the patch only when it inverts to exactly one change of existing
source terms:

- by a property rule: the decided value, with the property the source uses for that
  namespace;
- by a type rule: the decided type added, the other removed;
- by an edge-type rule from one source class.

A decision that requires a record absent from the source, or that comes from a rule with a
join or from a derived statement, is kept in the target-model result and listed in the
report as not writable in the source's terms. Only what the rules touch is covered: the
patch is applied to the source and never replaces it. For a table, the patch is applied to
the RDF produced by the mapping, and the affected rows are listed in the report.

The target-model graph is converted from the source and the patch by the same rules, and is
checked against the profile's SHACL shapes and by the target model's own validator.

Constraints learned from errors
-------------------------------

In shape extraction, a constraint is kept when enough of the data supports it, so that
constraints caused by wrong data are dropped (QSE, Rabbani et al. 2023; sheXer,
Fernández-Álvarez et al. 2022); the records behind such constraints are found by SHACTOR.
Authority-backed decisions provide what these methods lack: confirmed errors. The support
threshold can be set from them, and rare patterns can be identified as mistakes, so that a
shape is closed (``sh:closed``) where the patterns it excludes are confirmed wrong.

- Only constraints on the source's own data (counts, datatypes, identifier patterns, types
  that cannot hold together, closed shapes) belong in its shapes. Consistency with an
  authority is a rule, not a shape.
- A constraint is derived from a pattern of errors, not from a single error.
- Some apparent errors follow from the source's design. Learned constraints are therefore
  proposals to the source's maintainers (claim nanopublications), not facts.

Validation
----------

No artifact is signed or published during validation; each run is stored locally. Use cases:

- **U1**: WikiPathways, with UniProt, Rhea and ChEBI.
- **U2**: an interactome table mapped with RML, with NCBI Gene or HGNC, and UniProt.
- **U3**: AOP-Wiki chemicals, with ChEBI, and PubChem.

The approach is considered valid when:

- no case-specific code is needed beyond each case's rules and policy;
- every rule gives the same results on two engines;
- an application is run again from its nanopublications without rdfsolve.

Decisions
---------

.. list-table::
   :header-rows: 1
   :widths: 30 35 35

   * - Question
     - Decision
     - Reason
   * - What is a nanopublication
     - Rules, mapping claims, policies, applications and decisions; bulk results are a
       pinned dataset
     - One claim per statement does not scale to an interactome and adds no meaning;
       protocol, instructions and execution are separate levels (Celebi et al. 2020)
   * - Rule format
     - SPARQL executables; RML (from YARRRML) for tables
     - Run by any engine; used for SIB's collection of queries; RML has several engines
   * - Negative statements
     - Only under a complete answer and a stated uniqueness assumption
     - Cross-references are not exclusive; silence is not absence
   * - Policy
     - A nanopublication of its own, cited by decisions
     - Runs under different assumptions are visibly different
   * - Identity in rules
     - Joined through a mapping-set input
     - Identity is held in one place (SSSOM); rules stay plain SPARQL
   * - Completeness and evidence
     - DQV for each batch; ECO; semapv for mappings
     - Existing vocabularies; no new terms
   * - Place of comparison
     - The target model, after each source's rule
     - Authority rules are reused for every dataset
   * - Conflict
     - A violation of the target shapes
     - No conflict exists in RDF by itself; SHACL is standard and can be run again
   * - Result
     - A partial patch held as data, with a generated SPARQL Update
     - Removals are needed, and are not expressed by RML; data can be queried and compared
   * - SeMRA
     - Used through SSSOM, not as a dependency
     - See related tools
